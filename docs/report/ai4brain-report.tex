\section{Introduction}

AI now talks to us all day, and it is persuasive: in a preregistered study, GPT-4 with a few personal facts won 64\% of the debates that had a clear winner~\cite{salvi}. Meanwhile most ``brain training'' products make two promises they cannot keep: that a score is precise, and that practice makes a brain better. We set out to build the opposite: a coach that \emph{measures honestly}, \emph{explains with evidence}, and \emph{trains people to judge AI}, while treating the data of people's thinking as the sensitive data it is.

AI4Brain rests on three pillars, each a section of this report:
\begin{itemize}
\item \aitag{prob}{Measurement} (Section~\ref{sec:measure}): seven skills, explicit scoring formulas, a Bayesian profile with honest uncertainty, and active learning to choose the next game.
\item \aitag{gen}{Artificial intelligence} (Section~\ref{sec:ai}): eight AI components, from a language-model coach that cannot invent a number to an on-device face model that only asks ``are the player's eyes on the screen?''.
\item \aitag{guard}{Security and privacy} (Section~\ref{sec:security}): no email or password, per-user encryption with crypto-shredding, passkeys, hashed sessions, a tamper-evident log and brute-force limits we can prove.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:research} describes the research behind each decision, Section~\ref{sec:validation} the evidence that it works, and Section~\ref{sec:limits} what is still to do.

\begin{findbox}[title={Our own investigations}]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em]
\item \textbf{A validation study} on 500 simulated users with known abilities, run through the real pipeline (7 experiments, Section~\ref{sec:validation}). Its first run exposed miscalibrated bands (52--82\% coverage), which we fixed by fitting a measurement model per level.
\item \textbf{A router evaluation} comparing keyword rules with a language model on indirect wording (Table~\ref{tab:router}): the model understood 4 messages the rules missed.

A \emph{game} (internally a \emph{block}) is one run of one task at one difficulty level, about a minute. Every trial stores the stimulus, the response, its correctness, its response time measured from the frame that painted the stimulus, and whether the player looked away (Focus Guard). The engine scores the game and updates the Brain Twin; the selector picks the next game; the coach explains. The stack is Next.js~15 and TypeScript, Drizzle ORM on PostgreSQL (embedded PGlite for demos), Zod validation at every boundary, and either Claude or an open-weight model on Groq behind one interface.

\section{Measurement: the maths}
\label{sec:measure}

\subsection{Skills and tasks}
Six widely studied skills are measured with classic paradigms; a seventh, \emph{AI reliance}, is a collaboration skill (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). Each has a \emph{measurement card}, generated from its configuration, stating what it is, what it is \emph{not}, its levels, scoring, uncertainty model, data-quality checks and known biases.

\subsection{From trials to a game score}
Each task's metrics $m_k$ are normalised with configured bounds and combined into a composite
\begin{equation}
c=\sum_k w_k\,\tilde m_k,\qquad \tilde m_k=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{m_k-a_k}{b_k-a_k},0,1\right),\qquad \sum_k w_k=1 .
\end{equation}
$N$-back sensitivity uses the log-linear correction for extreme proportions~\cite{hautus}, and conflict costs are proportional to the person's own speed:
\begin{equation}
d'=\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{\text{hits}+0.5}{\text{targets}+1}\right)-\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{\text{false alarms}+0.5}{\text{lures}+1}\right),\qquad
I=\frac{\overline{RT}_{\text{hard}}-\overline{RT}_{\text{easy}}}{\overline{RT}_{\text{easy}}} .
\end{equation}
Each difficulty level $\ell$ covers a calibrated ability range, and the game becomes one observation $x=\text{lo}_\ell+(\text{hi}_\ell-\text{lo}_\ell)\,c$ on a common 0--1 scale, so a strong 3-back counts for more than an equally good 1-back.

\subsubsection{Data quality} A game is flagged when it has too few valid trials, accuracy not above chance (one-sided binomial test), long identical-answer streaks, many anticipations or timeouts, repeated focus loss, or more than 25\% of trials played looking away. A flagged game still counts, with its noise multiplied by $\kappa=2.5$.

\subsection{The Brain Twin}
Each skill is a Normal belief $\theta\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ starting from an explicit prior ($\mu_0=0.5$, $\sigma_0=0.2$). A game is an observation with noise $\sigma_x=\sigma_{\text{obs}}\sqrt{n_{\text{ref}}/n_{\text{valid}}}$ (times $\kappa$ if flagged), and the update is the precision-weighted, one-dimensional Kalman rule
\begin{equation}
\mu'=\frac{\mu/\sigma^2+x/\sigma_x^2}{1/\sigma^2+1/\sigma_x^2},\qquad \sigma'^2=\left(\sigma^{-2}+\sigma_x^{-2}\right)^{-1},\qquad \sigma^2(t+\Delta t)=\sigma^2(t)+q^2\Delta t,
\end{equation}
where the drift $q=0.02$ per day lets ability change between sessions. The user sees $\mu$ and the band $\mu\pm1.645\sigma$ in words (``fairly sure it is between 55 and 68''). A goal $g$ counts as reached only when the whole band is above it; until then the app shows $P(\theta\ge g)=1-\Phi((g-\mu)/\sigma)$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\fig{\linewidth}{twin_update}
\caption{One skill's estimate for a simulated user: the band narrows with each game and widens during a six-day gap.}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Active learning}
In \emph{Measure me} mode, for every skill, task and level the selector computes the expected narrowing of the band
\begin{equation}
\Delta\sigma=\sigma-\left(\sigma^{-2}+\tilde\sigma_x^{-2}\right)^{-1/2},
\end{equation}
with $\tilde\sigma_x$ inflated where a floor or ceiling is likely, halves the value for the skill just played, and plays the maximum. Each choice is explained (``We're least sure about your attention so far ($\pm$33\%). One Stroop game at level 2 should bring that to about $\pm$18\%.''), and assessment stops once every band is narrower than about $\pm$13 points. In \emph{Just practise} mode, the weakest skill is trained at the level with about 75\% expected success, then a 1-up/1-down staircase follows. A weekly plan ranks skills by $0.6\,(\text{band}/\text{prior band})+0.4\,(1-\mu)$, and a Bayesian linear trend gives each skill a 7-day forecast \emph{range} whose accuracy the dashboard later checks.

\subsubsection{One interface, two providers} Every language-model call goes through one module with two interchangeable back ends, Claude (Anthropic) or the open-weight \code{gpt-oss-120b} on Groq, always with strict JSON-schema output. Calls never throw: any failure returns a reason and the caller switches to its rule-based fallback, so the product works with no AI provider at all.

\subsubsection{Compute} The engine builds an \emph{evidence pack}, the only material the writer may use: scores with bands, the last six games with reliability, grouped trial facts, exact ``what it would take'' recalculations, forecasts at three paces, and up to 8 of 13 reviewed research findings. Items carry short references (R1, T3, W2, P1, S4); no name or database id leaves the server. The last 12 messages and a small memory (goal, favourite skill, recent topics) keep the conversation coherent.

\subsubsection{Write} The writer follows explicit rules (cite a reference per claim, use only pack numbers, mention the band, compare only with the user's own past, no medical terms, three sentences at most) and returns JSON: an intent, a reply, 0--3 cited claims, up to 3 action buttons and Bao's mood.

\subsubsection{Check} In code, with no second model: every reference must map to a real game or trial group, or the claim is dropped; every number must match the evidence that claim cites within rounding ($\pm1$ above 10, $\pm0.05$ below) or be a difference of two such numbers, after dates are removed; diagnostic terms and comparisons with others are removed, and health questions receive a fixed, kind, non-clinical answer; buttons must name real skills. This deterministic chain-of-verification~\cite{cove} cannot itself hallucinate, and the user can repeat it by tapping ``Why?''. Without consent or provider, a template coach answers the same intents through the same checks.

\subsection{Training people to judge AI}

\subsubsection{Second opinion} The player picks the larger of two sums, sees a \emph{simulated} AI helper's answer (described as ``sometimes wrong on purpose''), then decides again. Only disagreements count~\cite{schemmer}:
\begin{equation}
\text{AR}=\tfrac12\Big(P(\text{keep}\mid\text{right first},\text{AI wrong})+P(\text{switch}\mid\text{wrong first},\text{AI right})\Big),
\end{equation}
so ``always follow'' and ``always keep'' both land at 0.5.

\subsubsection{Resist the Persuader} After the player answers a surprising question, the live model argues, half the time for the truth with checkable evidence, half the time for the wrong answer with one named technique (fake expert, false precision, bandwagon, vivid story, cherry-picking). The server chooses the side and overrides the model's label for true sides. The reveal names the technique, and the score is the \emph{discernment} rate on challenged rounds, $D=\#\{\text{final correct}\wedge\text{AI disagreed}\}/\#\{\text{AI disagreed}\}$.

\subsubsection{Fabrication Hunt} Three checked facts are shown word for word, and the model invents one plausible but unambiguously false sentence, which the server places at random. The player picks it and wagers 1--3 tokens; the wager gap $G=\mathbb E[w\mid\text{caught}]-\mathbb E[w\mid\text{missed}]$ shows whether confidence tracks accuracy.

\subsubsection{AI Collaboration Score} 19 concrete signals (opening evidence, follow-ups, predictions before asking, in-scope questions, catching a deliberate error, the three games) feed five components weighted as in the brief, with Bayesian smoothing:
\begin{equation}
\text{score}=\operatorname{round}\Big(100\sum_c w_c\,s_c\Big),\qquad s_c=\frac{\sum_{i\in c}v_i+0.5\cdot2}{n_c+2},\qquad w=(0.20,0.25,0.20,0.20,0.15).
\end{equation}

A notification glanced at mid-game silently drags a score down. Focus Guard asks the camera one question: \emph{were the player's eyes on the screen?} Google's MediaPipe Face Landmarker~\cite{mediapipe} runs in the browser as WebAssembly, served by the application itself so the strict content-security policy holds. Ten times a second, head yaw and pitch come from the model's facial transformation matrix,
\begin{equation}
\text{yaw}=\operatorname{atan2}\!\big(-r_{20},\sqrt{r_{00}^2+r_{10}^2}\big),\qquad \text{pitch}=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{21},r_{22}),
\end{equation}
and eye direction from its eye-look blendshapes. A frame is \emph{away} when no face is found, $|\text{yaw}|>28^\circ$, $|\text{pitch}|>22^\circ$, or an eye-look score exceeds 0.65 (looking down at the keyboard counts half). Spells under 400\,ms are ignored; a trial is \emph{away} if its live window overlaps a spell, and away trials are excluded like too-fast answers. After 1.5\,s of continuous looking away, a banner (``Eyes back on the game'') and a soft two-note chime remind the player, with a mute option. It is off by default with its own consent, the app works identically without it, and it never infers emotion, fatigue or identity.

\section{Security and privacy}
\label{sec:security}

A tool that measures how people think holds sensitive data, so security is designed in, not added. Table~\ref{tab:threats} maps each threat to its defence.

\subsection{Accounts without personal data}
AI4Brain never asks for an email, a password or a real name. Signup offers a \textbf{passkey} first (WebAuthn, implemented on Node's own crypto: ES256, EdDSA and RS256 with strict CBOR parsing), so most people sign in with a fingerprint, face or device PIN. A random 80-bit \textbf{backup key} is shown once, can be saved to a password manager, downloaded or printed, and is stored only as a SHA-256 hash. A signed-in device can show a QR link or a 10-character code to sign in another one.

\subsection{Envelope encryption and crypto-shredding}

Each personal field is stored as $C=\operatorname{AES\text{-}GCM}_{K_u}(m;\ \text{iv},\ \text{aad}=\text{field})$~\cite{gcm}, with the user key wrapped as $W_u=\operatorname{AES\text{-}GCM}_{M_v}(K_u)$. Binding the field name as associated data stops ciphertexts being swapped between columns. Game scores stay in clear: the profiler needs them and they identify no one.

\subsection{Sessions and the security log}
Sessions are opaque random tokens, stored hashed, with idle and absolute expiry. Security events (sign-ins, new devices, consent changes, deletions) form a chain $h_i=\operatorname{HMAC}_k(h_{i-1}\,\|\,e_i)$, so any edit is detectable while personal details inside events can still be erased; Settings shows ``the security log is intact''.

\subsection{Brute force, with numbers}
Every typed secret is behind a progressive limit: after 3 free failures the wait doubles, $d_n=\min(250\,\text{ms}\cdot2^{\,n-3},\,8\,\text{s})$, and 10 failures lock the address for 15 minutes. Addresses come only from hops added by declared proxies (a client cannot forge them), IPv6 is grouped by /64, and a server-wide cap of 120 failed guesses a minute bounds distributed attacks; passkeys are exempt, so a flood never locks their users out. Even ignoring every limit, with $N$ users and $G$ guesses the chance of hitting any backup key is
\begin{equation}
P\le\frac{N\,G}{2^{80}}\approx10^{-10}\ \text{per day at 1{,}000 guesses/s and }N=10^6,
\end{equation}
and a device code, live for 5 minutes, is hit with probability about $3\times10^{-10}$ at the same rate.

Early user testing gave a blunt verdict, ``good as a concept, zero as user experience'', and the experience was rebuilt around one companion and three rules: plain words, one clear next step, no dark patterns. \section{Ethics}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-clinical.} A clinical-language filter, a fixed answer to health questions, a ``what it is not'' on every card, no population norms, and claims limited to practice on these tasks.
\item \textbf{Consent for every sensor and model.} The coach's models, the camera and the microphone each have their own opt-in consent, and the app is fully usable with all of them off. An 18+ step at signup.
\item \textbf{Honest AI.} Answers are labelled ``Written with AI, checked against your games''; the AI Lab says the model is sometimes wrong on purpose; research-mode errors are always revealed.
\item \textbf{Accessibility.} Every chart has a table view, the palette is checked for colour-vision deficiency, and a WCAG AAA high-contrast theme is available.
\end{itemize}

% ================================================================ 10
\section{Limitations and next steps}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simulated, not human, validation.} Next: a pilot of about 30 people over two sessions, to refit calibration and measure test--retest reliability.
\item \textbf{AI after data.} With consented real conversations, a small open model can be distilled from verified coach outputs; with real players, a learning game selector and skills that inform each other can outperform today's rules.
\item \textbf{Focus Guard} thresholds need tuning on real faces, glasses and lighting, laptops first, then phones.
\item \textbf{The router} has been evaluated on a small set, and does not yet read the conversation history for follow-up questions.
\item \textbf{Live-AI content.} The AI Lab's truth is fixed, but an invented ``fake'' could occasionally be accidentally true; reported rounds will go to a review queue.
\item \textbf{Language and device.} Stroop needs fluent English colour words, and response times depend on the device; users are compared only with themselves.
\end{itemize}
